## What the method does

The `[AL]` prompt is a single sentence, and it is a **question**:

> `[AL] Ask why the law of force between electrostatic charge was discovered after that of gravity if gravity is weak compared to electrostatic forces.`

It is not extra content — it is a **reverse question**: if the electric force is so much stronger, why did
its law arrive a century later? The textbook gives **no answer**; the practice is the pause, not the conclusion.

### Scaffolds you can offer (not from the textbook)

> [!WARNING]
> These three are **my guesses**, not the textbook's. Say so when using them.

| Possible angle | Rough direction |
|---|---|
| **An invisible force** | gravity shows itself as "things fall"; electric forces are masked by macroscopic neutrality |
| **Magnitudes and instruments** | Coulomb used a torsion balance — **no measurement, no quantitative law** |
| **Conceptual prerequisites** | "charge" itself had to be separable as net charge first |

## Conditions

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level | **AL** (tagged `[AL]`) |

| Load | high — an open question with no standard answer |

## Contraindications

| Not suitable for | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who have **not** mastered Coulomb's law itself | the question presupposes the magnitude comparison | **strong** |
| classrooms that need a standard answer to move on | the textbook gives none; treating a guess as the answer turns an open question into a fill-in-the-blank | **strong** |
| students outside AL | the `[BL] [OL]` prompt asks only "discuss later", without the "strong yet later" inference | **medium** |

## Risks

| Where it goes wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| presenting "no instruments" as **the** answer | the textbook gives none; a single answer kills the question |
| drifting into history of science | the function is a collision between magnitude intuition and historical fact |
| answering it yourself when students stall | the prompt says `Ask` — the question **is** the practice |
