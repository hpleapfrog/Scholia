## Method

The textbook's `[OL]` hints in this module give almost no derivation; instead they **repeatedly require making "who did what" explicit**.

### 1 · Tell one equation as the division of labour of three people

> `This equation illustrates the difference between Kepler's and Newton's work.`
> `Ask the students to explain why this is so.`

**What the textbook asks for is "have the students explain", not "the teacher explains it once."** The function of this step is to make
`F = GmM/r²` **not a formula that suddenly appears**, but an **explanation of an existing pattern**.

### 2 · Place the three people in chronological order

> `Compare the contributions of Kepler, Newton, and Einstein. Place them historically with dates.`

The three people correspond to three things (the `[AL]` hint gives the pairwise distinctions, see another teaching method):

| Person | What they gave |
|---|---|
| Kepler | **the pattern** (three laws) |
| Newton | **the explanation** (the law of universal gravitation) |
| Einstein | **a correction to the explanation** (general relativity) |

### 3 · Create the doubt first, then give the name

> `Ask students if it really is obvious why all things fall straight down.`
> `Ask them to back up their reasons. Ask if the name Halley rings a bell.`

**Note the order**: first have students **defend their own reasons**, then introduce Halley.

### 4 · The one confusion handled in a single line

The whole module's `[OL]` hint about `G` vs `g` is only one sentence:

> `Be sure no one is confusing G with g.`

**It treats this confusion as something that will happen by default** — so this is a **check item**, not an exposition.

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **OL** (textbook tag `[OL]`) |
| Prerequisites | has studied Kepler's three laws; knows ellipses and foci |
| Cognitive load | medium — the load sits on **"who explained what"**, not on the algebra |

## Contraindications

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who **have not yet studied Kepler's laws** | the whole force of this method lies in the contrast of "explaining vs being explained" — **without the former, there is no contrast** | **strong** (follows from the nature of the method) |
| students who need to be fluent with the formula before talking history | this method does not produce problem-solving fluency | **medium** |
| students **outside** this tier (OL) | the textbook tags exactly that passage `[OL]`; the BL and AL tiers get different suggestions | **medium** — the tag clearly draws the scope, but "not tagged" still ≠ "does not apply", it is merely **untested** |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| telling it as "Newton was great" | the textbook's landing point is **"this equation explains Kepler's pattern"** — a **logical relation**, not an assessment of a person |
| giving the formula directly and then adding "Kepler discovered it first" | this reverses the order. The textbook requires **pattern first, explanation second** |
| finishing after lining up the three dates | the dates are lined up so that the "correction" layer becomes visible too (there is Einstein after Newton) |
| mentioning `G` vs `g` once without checking | the textbook uses `Be sure no one is confusing` — the tone is **checking**, showing that merely telling once does not land |
