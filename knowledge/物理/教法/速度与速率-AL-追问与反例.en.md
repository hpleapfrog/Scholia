## Method

Two parts: **let students guess first, then knock the intuition out with a counterexample.**

### 1 · Question-based contrast

Point out that velocity, like displacement, is a **vector**, then let students **guess first** where velocity and speed differ, and deepen it with follow-up questions:

> `Why do you think that? What is an example? How might you apply these terms to motion that you see every day?`

Textbook original (short quotation):

> `Explain to students that velocity, like displacement, is a vector quantity.
> Ask them to speculate about ways that speed is different from velocity.
> After they share their ideas, follow up with questions that deepen their thought process`

### 2 · The average-speed counterexample (the textbook brings its own numbers)

> `Caution students that average speed is not always the average of an object's initial and final speeds.`

The textbook's example: a car travels 100 km — the first 50 km at 30 km/h, the last 50 km at 60 km/h.

- over **equal distances** the average speed = **40 km/h** (not (30+60)/2 = 45)
- if instead it drives **equal times** at 30 and 60 km/h respectively, only then is the average **45 km/h**

## Contraindications

**Who does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students outside this tier | the textbook **gives a different method for each tier** and does not state that this one carries across tiers | **weak** — “the textbook did not specify” ≠ “not applicable”, it is merely **untested** |

> [!WARNING]
> **This entry has no population contraindication backed by strong evidence.**
>
> The textbook offers its suggestion only for this tier and **does not say the method is ineffective for other tiers**.
> Treating “the textbook did not mention it” as “not applicable” is **over-inference** — so it is honestly marked **weak**, nothing is invented.

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **taking “average” as the arithmetic mean** | the textbook explicitly names this as the error to guard against: `average speed is not always the average of an object's initial and final speeds` | textbook, same section, `[OL][AL]` |
| giving the counterexample but not the general form | students remember the number 40, and it fails again with a different distance or speed. You must come back to `total distance / total time` | — |

> [!TIP]
> The value of this counterexample lies in showing **both** cases at once (equal distances 40 vs equal times 45).
> Give only one, and students will think “average speed never equals the arithmetic mean” — **which turns into another misconception**.
