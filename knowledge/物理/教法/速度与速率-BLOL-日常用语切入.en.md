## Method

**First have students voice their own everyday usage, then point out the gap between it and the scientific definitions.**

1. before reading the text, ask first: **where have you heard the word "speed" used**?
2. then ask: **have you heard the word "velocity"**?
3. name it: in everyday language these two words are **often used interchangeably**, but **their scientific definitions differ**
4. tell students: the difference will be covered in the text — **create suspense first, don't rush to give the answer**

Textbook original (short quotation):

> `Before students read the section, ask them to give examples of ways they have heard the word speed used.`
> `Then ask them if they have heard the word velocity used. Explain that these words are often used`
> `interchangeably in everyday life, but their scientific definitions are different.`

> [!WARNING]
> **This entry has no population contraindications backed by strong evidence.**
>
> The textbook only gives suggestions for this tier, **it does not say this method is ineffective for other tiers**.
> Treating "the textbook did not mention it" as "does not apply" is **over-inference** — here it is honestly marked **weak**, nothing is fabricated.

## Risks

The textbook itself gives one, and **both tiers share it within the same section**:

| Where it can go wrong | Why | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **in everyday speech, "average speed" and "instantaneous speed" are also simply called "speed"** | the textbook warns: `the terms speed, average speed, and instantaneous speed are all often referred to simply as speed in everyday language` — **even the teacher's colloquial speech reinforces this confusion** | textbook, same section `[BL][OL]` |
| only saying "the definitions differ" without landing it | students remember "different" but cannot say **where** they differ. The difference has to be closed out through vector / direction | — |

> [!WARNING]
> The feature of this contraindication: **it targets classroom language itself, not the students.**
> If the teacher themselves casually uses "velocity" to mean speed, this method is done in vain.
