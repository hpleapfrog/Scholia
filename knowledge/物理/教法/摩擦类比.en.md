## Method

Compare **resistance** to **friction and air resistance**: it opposes the current, and the collisions of charges with atoms transfer energy to the conductor itself.

The benefit of explaining resistance this way: it explains **why resistance heats up**, which the water-pipe analogy cannot.

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| prerequisite level | has studied friction and energy dissipation |
| stage | introductory university / senior-high elective |
| what is analogised | friction, air resistance |
| language | Chinese or English both fine |

## Contraindications

> [!CAUTION]
> **A method has no right or wrong, only “right for whom, and under what conditions”.**
> Being unable to state the contraindications means the evidence is not yet sufficient.

| Who | Why |
|---|---|
| students who have not yet formed the concept “resistance is a property of the material” | friction is a property of the **contact surfaces**, whereas resistance is a property of the **material and geometry**. An analogy imposed in advance makes students think resistance depends on “how rough the surface of the wire is” |
| students learning superconductivity | the friction analogy implies “dissipation is inevitable”, whereas a superconductor has zero resistance and no dissipation. The analogy fails outright there |

## Risks

> [!CAUTION]
> **The following is not “who it does not apply to”, but “where this analogy itself goes wrong”.**

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| the analogy suggesting “resistance = roughness of the contact surface” | friction is a property of the **contact surfaces**; resistance is a property of the **material and geometry**. Students may go looking for “whether the surface of the wire is rough” |
| the analogy implying “energy dissipation is inevitable” | friction necessarily dissipates; resistance **need not** (superconductivity has zero resistance and no dissipation). At superconductivity the analogy fails outright |

## Source of the method

This analogy comes from OpenStax《College Physics 2e》§20.3, where the original describes resistance as
"crudely similar to friction and air resistance".

> [!NOTE]
> **This is not “a better analogy”, but “an analogy alongside the other”.**
> The water-pipe analogy ([kb:physics:method-water-pipe](水管类比.en.md)) is strong on **immediacy**,
> the friction analogy is strong on **explaining heating**. Each has its own applicable population and its own failure boundary —
> this is exactly an instance of axiom A2 “teaching methods must coexist”.
>
> **Further observation**: after wiring in OpenStax《Physics》(**senior high**, CC BY), it turns out
> **the same publisher's senior-high textbook uses the water-flow/pipe analogy, and only the college textbook uses the friction analogy**.
> This suggests that the choice of analogy may relate to **stage**, rather than to textbook preference —
> see [proposition: choice of analogy is stage-dependent](../命题/类比选择与学段相关.en.md).
> That hypothesis has a sample of only 2 books and **is not yet established**.

## Argument

### M1 · Adopted by mainstream textbooks (no attack yet)

- **Claim**: this analogy is a usable teaching device, already adopted by a publicly published textbook
- **Premise**: OpenStax《College Physics 2e》§20.3 explains resistance with friction and air resistance (ev-016)
- **Reasoning**: that a widely adopted textbook uses the analogy shows it is workable for a particular population; but textbook adoption does not constitute evidence of effectiveness (textbooks are a low grade)
