## Method

**Do not tell students what inertia depends on; use two carts and let them derive it themselves.**

Textbook original (short quotation):

> `Take two similar carts or trolleys with wheels. Place a heavy weight in one of them.
> Ask students which cart would require more force to change its state of motion.
> Ask students which would stay in motion longer if you were to set them in motion.
> Based on this discussion, have students speculate on what inertia may depend on.`

Steps:

1. two carts of the same kind, with a heavy weight placed in one of them
2. ask: **which cart would need a greater force to change its state of motion?**
3. then ask: **which one would keep going longer once set in motion?**
4. let students **speculate themselves**: what might inertia depend on

**Key point**: the two questions ask **two sides of the same thing** (hard to change / keeps going long);
students will run into the answer “mass” by themselves.

The same section's `[AL]` also gives two extensions: instances of the first law in everyday life;
and **drawing a force diagram that includes friction**, with emphasis on the direction of the friction force.

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| tier | **AL** (textbook marker `[AL]`) |
| prerequisite | able to propose and compare hypotheses |
| equipment | two carts of the same kind + one heavy weight (low cost) |
| cognitive load | high — the load sits on **inferring the variable from the phenomenon** |

## Contraindications

**Who does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who do not yet know what “state of motion” refers to | both questions take “changing the state of motion” as a shared premise; if the premise does not stand, there is nothing to compare | **medium** — inferred from the method |
| students outside this tier | the textbook **gives a different method for each tier** and does not state that this one carries across tiers | **weak** — untested |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| a student answers “the heavier one keeps going longer” but with the reason “heavier, so more solid” | it stays in everyday language and never rises to “mass is the measure of inertia”. The teacher needs to **translate it into a physical quantity** |
| treating “weight” as “mass” | the same section's `[OL]` explicitly requires distinguishing the two words (`may be used interchangeably in everyday life but have different meanings in science`) — **this method naturally invites that confusion** |
| uncontrolled experimental conditions | if the wheel friction of the two carts differs, the conclusion is contaminated. The textbook only says “similar”, not how to ensure it |
