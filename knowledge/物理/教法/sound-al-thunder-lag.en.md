## Method

**First have students guess the speed of sound, then overturn or confirm it with a phenomenon they have personally lived through.**

Textbook original (short quotation):

> `Ask students if they know the speed of sound and if not, ask them to take a guess.`
> `Ask them why the sound of thunder is heard much after the lightning is seen during storms.`
> `This phenomenon is also observed during a display of fireworks. Through this discussion,`
> `develop the concept that the speed of sound is finite and measurable and is much slower than that of light.`

Steps:

1. ask: **roughly what is the speed of sound?** (guess first; wrong guesses allowed)
2. ask: **why do you see the lightning before you hear the thunder during a storm?** (same with fireworks)
3. wrap up: the speed of sound is **finite and measurable**, and **much slower than the speed of light**

The same section's `[AL]` also has two extensions: **fret positions on instruments are placed at mathematically determined intervals** (the shorter the string, the faster it vibrates, the higher the pitch);
and **what would happen if the speed of sound varied with frequency** (a counterfactual follow-up).

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **AL** (textbook tag `[AL]`) |
| Prerequisites | students have personal experience of thunderstorms / fireworks |
| Cognitive load | high — the load sits in **overturning an intuition with a familiar phenomenon** |

## Contraindications

**Who does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students without thunderstorm or fireworks experience | this method rests entirely on a **shared experience**; without it the discussion has no starting point | **medium** — derived from the method |
| students outside this tier | the textbook **gives a different method for each tier** and does not state that this method works across tiers | **weak** — untested |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| students attribute "seen before heard" to "light is brighter so it arrives first" | the time gap has nothing to do with brightness. If not pressed, students may form a new wrong explanation |
| staying at "light is faster than sound" without landing on "the speed of sound is finite and measurable" | the textbook's goal is **finiteness** and **measurability**, not a single comparison |
| the counterfactual follow-up (speed of sound varying with frequency) taken as fact | the textbook's wording is `Ask students to predict` — **that is a hypothesis question, not a knowledge point** |
