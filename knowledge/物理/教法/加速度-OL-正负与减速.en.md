## Method

The textbook's `[OL]` hint **does not give the formula first**; it deals with two words first.

### 1 · Start from students' own examples

> `Begin a general discussion about acceleration and deceleration. Ask for examples of both.`
> `Lead students to their topics of interest, such as motor vehicles or sports.`

**Note that it asks for examples of both acceleration and deceleration** — first let students pour out all their everyday usage.

### 2 · Then say that the word "deceleration" is not used in physics

> `Explain that deceleration is not used in physics because acceleration is either`
> `positive or negative.`

**The function of this step is to stop "-2 m/s²" from being a synonym for "slowing down".**
The textbook **says it again** in a later hint (see step 4), which shows that saying it once is not enough.

### 3 · The correspondence between `Δ` and the subscripts must be made visible

> `Explain that the capital Greek letter delta always means final minus initial and that the net`
> `change may be zero, positive, or negative.`
> `Use the equation a ¯ = Δv Δt = v f − v 0 t f − t 0 to emphasize the relationship between`
> `Δ and the subscripts f and 0.`

**The textbook teaches through the written form itself**: put `Δv` and `v_f − v_0` side by side and the correspondence shows itself.

### 4 · Say that word once more, and assign the "negative" to the coordinate direction

> `Be sure students understand that the word deceleration is not used in physics and that`
> `acceleration may be either positive or negative.`

**The same hint carries two other things**:

> `Distinguish between constant and variable acceleration. There could be confusion here,`
> `especially in the case of increasing acceleration.`

("Increasing acceleration" is the easiest to confuse — **what changes is the acceleration itself, not the velocity**.)

> `Verify that the students know the SI units ... Explain the meaning of seconds squared`
> `in the denominator of the units of acceleration.`

(The denominator of `m/s²` **is not an area**.)

### 5 · Finally, and most important: the visual explanation with the reversed arrow

> `The arrow for acceleration that points opposite to the arrow for velocity may be confusing.`
> `Explain that the acceleration arrow points in the direction opposite the velocity because`
> `the velocity is getting smaller, i.e., the velocity arrow is getting shorter.`

**The textbook gives no new formula; it gives an action you can see** — **the velocity arrow is getting shorter**.

### 6 · Check three things before calculating

> `Before beginning the calculations, verify that students understand the equation for`
> `acceleration. Do they understand what it means when quantities have a plus or minus sign?`
> `Do they understand the units for each variable?`

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **OL** (textbook tag `[OL]`) |
| Prerequisites | displacement, velocity; can substitute into a formula and do one division |
| Cognitive load | medium — the difficulty is not the arithmetic but **the correspondence between sign and direction** |

## Contraindications

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who **have not yet established that "velocity has a direction"** | the whole force of step 5 of this method lies in **the directional relation between two arrows**; treat velocity as just a number and there is nothing to say here | **strong** — follows from the nature of the method |
| students who only want to plug into formulas and solve problems quickly | this method **spends time on wording first** and does not produce problem-solving speed | **medium** |
| students **outside** this tier (OL) | the textbook tags exactly that passage `[OL]`; the BL and AL tiers get different suggestions | **medium** — the tag clearly draws the scope, but "not tagged" still ≠ "does not apply", it is merely **untested** |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| using "deceleration" casually as loose colloquial speech | the textbook's wording is `is not used in physics` — **this is a terminological stipulation, not a style preference**. A teacher who uses "deceleration" themselves ruins this section |
| "negative acceleration = slowing down" | this is exactly the equation the textbook wants to break. **The sign depends on the coordinate direction**: forward motion with backward acceleration is slowing down, backward motion with backward acceleration is speeding up |
| "acceleration is getting bigger" told as "velocity is getting bigger" | the textbook specifically flags `especially in the case of increasing acceleration`. **What changes is the rate of change** |
| explaining the reversed arrow with a formula | the textbook gives `the velocity arrow is getting shorter` — **a visual statement**. Switching back to a formula makes a simple question complicated |
