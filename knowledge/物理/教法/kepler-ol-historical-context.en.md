## Method

The textbook puts **most of this section's `[OL]` teaching hints on "historical context"**, not on the formula.

### 1 · Explain how far ahead of almost everyone Kepler was

> `Discuss the historical setting in which Kepler worked. Most people still thought Earth was the
> center of the universe, and yet Kepler not only knew that the planets circled the sun,
> he found patterns in the paths they followed. What would it be like to be that far ahead of
> almost everyone?`

The textbook also names an available narrative resource (*Cosmos*, episode 3).

### 2 · Make the scale of his "crunching by hand" tangible

> `Impress upon the students that Kepler had to crunch an enormous amount of data and that
> all his calculations had to be done by hand. Ask students to think of similar projects where
> scientists found order in a daunting amount of data (the periodic table, DNA structure,
> climate models, etc.).`

### 3 · Draw ellipses by hand (pins and string)

The textbook gives a **pins + string** method for drawing ellipses, and then asks:

> `Why does the string and pin method create a shape that conforms to Kepler's second law?
> That is, why is the shape an ellipse?`

### 4 · The scope-of-application point easiest to miss (`[OL]`)

> `Emphasize that this approach only works for two satellites orbiting the same parent body.
> The parent body must be the same because r²/T² = GM/(4π²) and M is the mass of the parent body.
> If M changes, the ratio r³/T² also changes.`

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **OL** (textbook tag `[OL]`) |
| Prerequisites | can substitute into `r³/T²` for comparison; knows the major axis / minor axis / focus of an ellipse |
| Teaching aids | pins + string + pencil (low cost, usable in class or taken home) |
| Cognitive load | medium — the load sits in **restoring the formula into something that actually happened** |

## Contraindications

**Who does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who do not yet know the "same parent body" restriction | the textbook specifically emphasizes `only works for two satellites orbiting the same parent body` — **without naming it, students will compare across different parent bodies** | **strong** (the textbook explicitly states `Emphasize`) |
| students who just want to grab the formula and solve problems quickly | the time this method spends on history **does not produce problem-solving ability**; in exam-oriented short class periods the cost-effectiveness is low | **medium** — follows from the nature of the method |
| students outside this tier | the textbook **gives a different method for each tier** and does not state that this method works across tiers | **weak** — untested |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| history told as a story hour | the textbook's landing point is **"he was ahead of almost everyone"** and **"crunched by hand"**, both pointing at one thing: **this law was extraordinarily costly to obtain**. Drifting into anecdotes loses this function |
| the pins-and-string ellipse drawn without follow-up questions | the textbook explicitly asks **"why does this drawing method produce an ellipse"**. Drawing without asking degenerates into a crafts class |
| `r³/T²` used carelessly across different parent bodies | this is **the substantive error most easily made** in this section. The textbook marks it with `Emphasize`, showing it expects students to make it |
| the "law of areas" taught as "constant speed" | the textbook's statement is **equal areas swept in equal times**, while **both the speed and the distance are changing** |
