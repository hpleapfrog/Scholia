## Method

**Pin down the criterion with a set of counterintuitive examples, then let students keep producing examples themselves.**

Textbook original (short quotations):

> `Make it clear why holding something off the ground or carrying something over a level surface`
> `is not work in the scientific sense.`

> `Ask the students to provide more examples until they understand the difference between`
> `the scientific term work and a task that is simply difficult but not literally work`
> `(in the scientific sense).`

**Three steps:**

| # | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Put out two counterexamples: **holding something still**, **carrying something horizontally** |
| 2 | Have students **explain with the energy equation** why these two do not count as work |
| 3 | **Have students keep producing examples themselves** until they distinguish "work in the scientific sense" from "things that are simply tiring" |

The same section of the textbook has one more companion requirement: **make clear the separate units of work, energy, force and distance**,
and use the mechanical-energy equation and the work equation to **go through on the spot which things are work and which are not**.

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **OL** (textbook tag `[OL]`) |
| Prerequisites | already know the mechanical energy equation and `W = F·d` |
| Cognitive load | medium — the load sits in **using the criterion to overrule intuition** |

## Contraindications

**Who does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who do not yet have the mechanical energy equation | step 2 of the method is "**explain with the mechanical energy equation**"; without it they can only memorize the conclusion | **medium** — stated explicitly by the textbook |
| students outside this tier | the textbook **gives a different method for each tier** and does not state that this method works across tiers | **weak** — untested |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| students concluding **"tiring things do no work"** | the criterion is **force and displacement**, not tiredness. Running is of course tiring, but **running does work** (there is displacement and there is force). Turning a counterexample into a general rule is wrong |
| "holding something still does no work" taken as "no energy is consumed" | **the muscles really are consuming chemical energy** (muscle fibres contract repeatedly in micro-contractions). Work in the scientific sense being zero **does not mean the body consumed nothing** |
| stopping after only two counterexamples | the textbook explicitly requires `provide more examples **until** they understand` — **"until" means two examples are not enough** |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **The last risk is the crux of this entry.**
>
> "Holding something still does no work" puts students' intuition and physics at odds, because **the muscles really are burning energy**.
> If this layer is not named, students will either deny physics or deny their own feeling.
>
> **The right way to close the loop**: physical work is judged by **the effect of an external force**;
> where the energy consumed inside the muscles goes is **a question of physiology**, not a counterexample to this criterion.
