## Method

**Before teaching the speed of sound, first establish the premise that "sound is a mechanical wave".**

Textbook original (short quotation):

> `Review the fact that sound is a mechanical wave and requires a medium through which it is transmitted.`

A companion data observation (same section, `[BL]`):

> `Note that in the table, the speed of sound in very rigid materials such as glass, aluminum, and steel
> … is quite high, whereas the speed in rubber, which is considerably less rigid, is quite low.`

**Key**: students can work out for themselves from the table that "the stiffer the medium, the
higher the speed of sound".
**Do not give this rule first** — let students look at the data first.

## Conditions of use

| Dimension | Value |
|---|---|
| Level tier | **BL** (textbook marker `[BL]`) |
| Prerequisites | none (this practice is itself the prerequisite) |
| Teaching aids | a table of "the speed of sound in different media" |
| Cognitive load | low — the load is in **reading the rule out of the table** |

## Contraindications

**Who or what situations does it not apply to?**

| Who or situation it does not apply to | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who have not yet formed the concept of a "wave" | this practice's premise is "mechanical wave"; conclusions such as sound cannot travel through a vacuum all rest on it | **medium** — inferred from the practice |
| students outside this tier | the textbook gives **different practices** for each tier, and does not declare this practice transferable across tiers | **weak** — untested |

## Risks

| Where it can go wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| "the stiffer the medium, the higher the speed of sound" is taken as a universal law | this is a **data observation**, not a derived conclusion. Students may over-generalize it to all waves |
| memorizing "sound cannot travel through a vacuum" with no explanation | the conclusion is remembered but never connected to the cause "a mechanical wave needs a medium"; change the situation and they cannot use it |
