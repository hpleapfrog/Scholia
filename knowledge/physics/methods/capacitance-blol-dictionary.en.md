## What the method does

The section sets its own tone in the first sentence:

> `To present capacitors, this section emphasizes their capacity to store energy.`

**Not `C = Q/U` first — "how much can it hold" first.**

### 1. Look the word up instead of defining it

> `[BL] Have students define how the word capacity is used in everyday life. Have them look up the definition in the dictionary. Compare and contrast the everyday meaning with the meaning of the term in physics.`

Three actions: say it → look it up → **compare**. The comparison is the point: everyday "capacity"
and physical "capacitance" differ in **what is stored** — charge and energy, not "how much stuff fits".

### 2. Put energy storage next to other storage

> `To introduce the idea of energy storage, discuss with students other mechanisms of storing energy, such as dams or batteries. Ask which have greater capacity.`

The question is "which holds more", not "which is more advanced" — that turns "capacity" into
something comparable.

### 3. Name a word they may already have heard

> `[OL] Ask students whether they have heard the word capacitor used in conjunction with electricity, such as in power stations or electric circuits.`

## Contraindications

| Not suitable for | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| students who have not met **potential difference** | "stores charge and energy" needs `U` to be said precisely | **strong** |
| students who need `C = Q/U` or series/parallel calculations now | produces no calculation | **medium** |
| students outside BL/OL | `[AL]` uses a spring instead | **medium** |

## Risks

| Where it goes wrong | Why |
|---|---|
| translating `capacity` as 容量 and moving on | the textbook asks for **comparison**; a translation skips it |
| explaining capacitance with a water bucket | a bucket stores **matter**; a capacitor stores charge and energy — the analogy swaps what is stored |
| starting with `C = Q/U` | the order is storage → capacitance; reversed, "capacitance does not depend on charge" becomes unsayable |
